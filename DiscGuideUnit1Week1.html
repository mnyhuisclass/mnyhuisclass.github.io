Before We Begin

Key Vocabulary

Learn these words — they will appear on ILEARN! You will use them all week.

Energy
The ability to cause change or do work
Stored Energy
Energy held in an object that hasn't been released yet
Energy Transfer
When energy moves from one object to another
Collision
When two objects hit or crash into each other
Speed
How fast an object is moving
Motion Energy
Energy that a moving object has

Pre-teach vocabulary on Day 1 before the phenomenon. Post vocabulary on a word wall and refer to it throughout the week. For ELL students, pair each word with a visual or gesture. Encourage students to use these words during class discussions — specifically during sense-making conversations.

Day 1 — 30 Minutes

The Phenomenon: Crash!

🔍 Today's Phenomenon

Why does a faster car cause more damage in a crash?

Watch the crash test video your teacher shows you. Pay close attention to what happens to the car and the crash test dummy. Think like a scientist — what do you notice? What questions pop into your head?

👁 I Notice...
❓ I Wonder...

Sample student observations: The car crumbles at the front. The dummy gets thrown forward. The faster crash makes a bigger dent than the slow crash.

Sample student wonders: Why does going faster cause more damage? Does a heavier car cause more damage too? What protects the people inside?

Accept any reasonable, observable statements. Look for students connecting the speed of the car to the amount of damage — this is the core concept for 4-PS3-1.

Video suggestions (free): Search "IIHS slow vs fast car crash" on YouTube — the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety has excellent side-by-side crash comparisons at 25 mph vs 40 mph that make the speed-energy relationship immediately visible.

Facilitation tip: After watching, give 2 minutes of quiet think time before partners share. Cold call 3–4 students. Anchor the discussion with: "What changed between the slow crash and the fast crash?"

ILEARN connection: The ILEARN item format starts with a phenomenon exactly like this. Students need practice making observations and generating testable questions — don't skip this step!

  • Extension: Show a second phenomenon — a bowling ball slowly rolling into pins vs. a fast throw. Ask: "Is this the same pattern?"
  • Discussion Prompt: "If speed and energy are connected, how could engineers use that knowledge to make cars safer?"
  • Cross-curricular: Connect to ELA — informational text structures. The "Notice/Wonder" frame mirrors good reading comprehension habits.

💬 Discuss with your class:

Do you think a faster car causes more damage or a slower car? How could we test that idea in the classroom?

Day 2 — 30 Minutes

The Car Ramp Investigation

Materials per group: 2 toy cars (same car, different release heights), 1 ramp (ruler + 2–3 books), 1 lump of air-dry clay (golf ball size), 1 ruler, 1 recording sheet (this page). Set up ramps before class to save time.

Management tip: Assign clear roles — Materials Manager, Roller, Observer, Recorder. Rotate roles between trials.

🧪 Our Question

How does the speed of a toy car affect the size of the dent it makes in clay?

My Hypothesis (What I think will happen)

📋 Materials Checklist

🔢 Procedure — Follow These Steps

  • 1
    Set up your ramp low (slow speed).

    Stack 1 book under the end of your ruler. Place the clay flat at the bottom of the ramp, about 4 inches from the end.

  • 2
    Release the car — don't push it!

    Let go of the car from the top of the ramp. Watch carefully. Does it hit the clay?

  • 3
    Measure the dent.

    Use your finger to gently feel how deep the dent is. Describe it in words: small, medium, or large. Smooth out the clay for the next trial.

  • 4
    Raise the ramp high (fast speed).

    Stack 3 books under the ruler. Place the clay in the same spot. Release the car from the top.

  • 5
    Measure and compare the dent.

    How does the second dent compare to the first? Record your observations in the data table below.

  • 6
    Repeat both trials.

    Do each ramp height 2 times. Record all results in your data table.

Safety: Remind students to never push the car — gravity only. Make sure clay is flat and stable. Have students hold the ramp base so it doesn't slide.

What to look for: Groups where the high-ramp dent is NOT larger may have placed the clay too far away or pushed the car. Help them troubleshoot — this is good scientific practice!

Variable control: Ask students: "What things did we keep the SAME in both trials?" (same car, same clay spot, same release method). This prepares them for ILEARN questions about controlled variables.

  • Extend the investigation: Add a third ramp height (medium). Does the dent size increase predictably?
  • Advanced question: "What if we used a heavier car at the same height? Would the dent be different? Why?" (connects mass to energy — a preview of future learning)
  • Graphing extension: Have students sketch a bar graph: ramp height on the x-axis, dent size on the y-axis. This connects to CS standards (3-5.DI.2).

📊 Data Table — Record Your Results

Trial Ramp Height Car Speed Dent Size (Small/Medium/Large) Other Observations
1A Low (1 book) Slow
1B Low (1 book) Slow
2A High (3 books) Fast
2B High (3 books) Fast

Expected results:

  • Trials 1A & 1B (slow): Small dent — the car has less energy and transfers less force to the clay
  • Trials 2A & 2B (fast): Large dent — the car has more energy and transfers more force to the clay
  • Both trials within the same ramp height should produce similar (consistent) results

If results are inconsistent between repeats, prompt students to identify what might have varied (release point, clay position, etc.) — this is productive scientific reasoning.

What pattern do you see in your data?

When the car goes faster, the dent is larger. When the car goes slower, the dent is smaller. This pattern shows that faster-moving objects have MORE energy.

Day 4 — 30 Minutes

PhET Simulation: Energy Skate Park

🌐 Go to: phet.colorado.edu → Search "Energy Skate Park" → Click "Basics"

Setup: Pre-load the URL on student tablets before class. Use the "Basics" version (not the full simulation) — it's more accessible for 4th grade. Have students work in pairs — one controls, one records.

Teacher demo (first 5 min): Project on the board. Show how to release the skater from different heights. Ask: "What do you predict will happen to the skater's speed at the bottom when we release from higher up?"

Free account not required. Works in Chrome browser on most school tablets.

🛹 Exploration Tasks

Try each task and record what you observe:

Task 1: Release from LOW on the ramp

Release the skater from a LOW position. Watch the speed bar. How fast does the skater go at the bottom?

Task 2: Release from HIGH on the ramp

Now release from a HIGH position. What happens to the speed at the bottom? How is it different?

Task 3: Connect to our experiment

How is the skater simulation similar to our car ramp from Day 2? What is the same? What is different?

Task 1: The skater moves slowly at the bottom — less stored energy means less speed.

Task 2: The skater moves much faster at the bottom — more stored energy (from being higher up) converts to more motion energy.

Task 3: Similar — both show that starting from higher means more speed/energy at the bottom. Different — the simulation shows the energy bar visually; our car experiment showed the energy through the size of the dent in clay.

  • Friction exploration: In the full version of PhET Energy Skate Park, students can turn friction on/off. Ask: "What happens to the skater's energy when friction is added?" (connects to energy transfer as heat)
  • Science journal prompt: "Draw the energy bar at the top of the ramp vs. the bottom. Where is the energy stored? Where does it go?"
  • CS connection (3-5.DI): The PhET simulation uses data to represent energy. Ask: "What data does the computer need to show us the skater's energy?"

Day 5 — 30 Minutes

Reading: Energy Is Everywhere!

Energy Is Everywhere!
Science Thursday Reading Passage • Grade 4 • Unit 1

Have you ever watched a bowling ball roll down a lane and crash into the pins? Or seen a roller coaster zoom down a steep hill? These exciting events all have something in common — energy.

Scientists define energy as the ability to cause change. Energy is all around us, and it comes in many forms: sound, heat, light, and motion. But what determines how much energy a moving object has?

The answer is speed. The faster an object moves, the more energy it carries. Think about two soccer balls: one rolling slowly across the grass and one kicked hard across the field. If both balls hit a stack of cups, which one will knock more cups over? The faster ball will — because it has more energy.

This is exactly why car crashes at high speeds cause more damage than low-speed crashes. Engineers who design cars use this knowledge to create safety features like crumple zones and airbags. These features absorb the energy during a crash so that less energy reaches the people inside.

Energy doesn't just disappear when objects collide. It gets transferred — passed from one object to another. When a fast-moving car crashes into clay, the clay receives the car's energy and changes shape. The more energy transferred, the bigger the change.

Understanding energy and speed is one of the most important ideas in physical science — and it's something scientists and engineers use every single day!

Reading strategy: Use a "close reading" approach — first read for understanding, second read to identify evidence. Ask students to underline the main claim and circle evidence words (because, so, which means, therefore).

ELA integration: This passage is written at approximately a Lexile 760 level. For struggling readers, pair with a partner. For advanced readers, ask them to identify the author's purpose and evaluate whether the evidence supports the claims.

Vocabulary check: Before reading, point out bold words: energy, speed, transferred. After reading, ask: "Which vocabulary word from Day 1 is most important in this passage? Why?"

📋 Reading Graphic Organizer

1. What is the main idea of this passage?

The main idea is that the faster an object moves, the more energy it has, and this energy is transferred when objects collide.

2. Find TWO pieces of evidence from the passage that support this main idea. Write them below.
  • Evidence 1: The faster soccer ball will knock more cups over because it has more energy.
  • Evidence 2: Car crashes at high speeds cause more damage than low-speed crashes.
  • Accept any two pieces of text evidence that connect speed to energy or energy to damage/change.
3. How does the reading CONNECT to the car ramp experiment we did on Day 2?

The reading connects because it explains why our fast car made a bigger dent — because it had more energy. When the fast car hit the clay, it transferred more energy, causing a bigger change (larger dent). This is the same as what engineers use to design safer cars.

4. Circle one vocabulary word from the passage and write it in a new sentence:

Accept any sentence that uses a vocabulary word correctly in context (energy, speed, motion, transferred, collision). Look for evidence that the student understands the word's meaning, not just that they used it.

  • Writing extension: Have students write a second paragraph that could be added to the passage — explaining what they discovered in their own car ramp experiment.
  • Science-ELA connection: Ask students to identify the text structure of the passage (cause and effect). How does this text structure match the science concept?
  • Research connection: "The passage mentions engineers use energy knowledge to design airbags. Use a tablet to find one more safety feature in cars and explain how it uses energy science."

End of Week — ILEARN Practice

Exit Ticket

⭐ ILEARN-Style Question

A student rolls a bowling ball slowly down a lane. It hits one pin and knocks it over. Then the student rolls the ball much faster. What will most likely happen — and WHY?

Use your data and vocabulary as evidence:

Full credit response: The faster bowling ball will knock down more pins (or all of them). This will happen because a faster-moving object has more energy. When the fast ball collides with the pins, it transfers more energy to them, causing greater change (more pins knocked over). Our data showed this same pattern — the fast car made a much larger dent in the clay than the slow car because it had more energy to transfer.

Scoring guide:

  • ✓ Correct prediction (more pins knocked over) — 1 point
  • ✓ Mentions speed and energy connection — 1 point
  • ✓ Mentions energy transfer during collision — 1 point
  • ✓ Cites evidence from experiment or reading — 1 point

Common misconception: Students may say "the heavier ball has more energy." Redirect: "In our experiment, we only changed the HEIGHT (speed). What variable was responsible for the change we saw?"

After collecting exit tickets: Sort into 3 piles — Got it, Getting there, Not yet. Use this data at your weekly co-planning session to decide who needs small group support before Week 2. Students who "Got it" can serve as peer partners in the first day of Week 2.

ILEARN task demand addressed: Task Demand 4 — "Use an explanation to predict how the expression of energy will change given a change in speed." This is exactly the type of question students will see in an ILEARN item cluster.

⚠️ ILEARN Content Limits

  • Students do NOT need to calculate speed or energy numerically
  • Students do NOT need to know "kinetic energy" or "potential energy" — these terms are NOT expected
  • Questions will use qualitative/comparative language: faster/slower, more/less energy, larger/smaller change
  • Students ARE expected to know: energy, collision, speed, stored energy, energy transfer, forms of energy (sound, heat, light, motion)
